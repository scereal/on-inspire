Borrowed teaching moves

Ten ideas from teachers, schools and creators who are famous for making hard maths feel obvious. Each one is rebuilt here as a small working demo, so you can feel the move behind it, then remix it into something of your own. Below them: a workbench of analogies, with the place each one breaks.

    Steal the move, not the artworkEach exhibit names the transferable technique. The demos are fresh builds of the idea, credited and linked, so you study why it works rather than copy how it looks.

    Try it before readingPlay with each demo first. Notice the moment it clicks for you. That moment is what the notes try to name.

    Then remixEvery exhibit ends with prompts: the same move pointed at a different concept. Those are your raw material.

    01Turn the shape into one you already know

    Peel a circle into a triangle

    After Grant Sanderson, 3Blue1Brown, “The essence of calculus”

    Why it works

    Nobody needs to be told the area formula. Slice the circle into rings, unroll each ring into a thin strip of length 2πr, and stack the strips by size. The strips make a staircase that becomes a triangle with base R and height 2πR, so the area is ½ · R · 2πR = πR². The derivative sneaks in for free: each new ring adds 2πr of area per unit of radius, so (πr²)′ = 2πr.

    Analogies to try

    Peeling an onion and laying the layers flat on a cutting board. Breaks: onion layers have thickness that doesn't shrink to zero.
    A roll of tape: the area of the roll is the area of the tape unwound. Breaks: fine for area, but tape has a cardboard hole in the middle.

    Remix prompts

    • Which other formula hides a “layers” story? The sphere's volume, built from shells of area 4πr², is the 3D sequel.
    • Could a learner predict the triangle's height before seeing it?
    • What does the staircase's overshoot look like, and when is it a lesson about error?

    02Follow two arrows and everything else follows

    A matrix is where the grid goes

    After Grant Sanderson, 3Blue1Brown, “Essence of linear algebra”

    Why it works

    Drag the teal and gold arrow tips. Their landing spots are the two columns of the matrix, and the whole grid follows because gridlines stay parallel and evenly spaced. The determinant stops being a formula to memorise: it's how much the shaded unit square's area grew. It goes negative when the square gets flipped over, and hits zero when space is squashed flat.

    Analogies to try

    A matrix is a recipe that only says where two ingredients go; linearity cooks the rest. Breaks: only for linear maps; a translation isn't in the recipe.
    Stretching a printed grid on a sheet of rubber, pinned at the origin. Breaks: rubber can bend lines; a linear map can't.
    The determinant as an exchange rate for area: every square metre becomes |det| square metres.

    Remix prompts

    • Which vectors only get stretched, never turned? Let learners hunt for eigenvectors by dragging.
    • Show two transformations in a row. Why does order matter?
    • Turn “det = 0 means no inverse” into a puzzle: can you unsquash a line?

    03Exaggerate the numbers until intuition wakes up

    Monty Hall, with a hundred doors

    After Lisa Goldberg (UC Berkeley) on Numberphile, building on the classic puzzle

    Stay
    Switch

    Why it works

    With 3 doors, “two doors left, so it's 50–50” feels airtight. With 100, it collapses: you picked one door at 1%, the host, who knows where the car is, opens 98 goats, and one door is conspicuously left shut. Your door is still 1%. The other door carries the remaining 99%. Same logic, but the exaggeration makes the asymmetry impossible to miss.

    Analogies to try

    The host is a filter, not a coin: he hands you the best of 99 doors. Breaks: if the host opens doors at random and happens to reveal goats, switching no longer helps. Knowledge is the whole point.
    Your first pick versus “everything else, tidied up for you”.

    Remix prompts

    • Where else does pushing a parameter to an extreme reveal the answer? Try the birthday problem with 365 people, or the limit of a sum.
    • Let learners vote before the 1,000-game run, then compare their vote with the bars.
    • Build the “random host” version and ask why the answer changes.

    04Let the wrong idea speak first, then break it

    A rope around the Earth

    The move comes from Derek Muller's PhD research behind Veritasium: clear explanations left misconceptions intact, while videos that confronted them produced bigger gains. The puzzle itself is a classic.

    A rope is pulled tight around the equator (40,075 km). Add 1 metre to its length and lift it evenly all the way round. How high off the ground does it float?

    Why it works

    Most people guess something microscopic: 1 metre spread over 40,000 km. The answer, 1/(2π) m ≈ 15.9 cm, is shocking, and the shock is the lesson. Circumference is 2πr, so adding 1 to the circumference adds 1/(2π) to the radius, whatever the planet. Muller found that a learner who first commits to the wrong idea and then sees it fail learns more than one handed a tidy explanation, even though the confrontation feels more confusing in the moment.

    Analogies to try

    Running a lap on the outside lane of a track: the extra distance depends only on how far out you are, not on how big the track is. Breaks: tracks have straights; the rope is a pure circle.
    Circumference and radius are on a fixed exchange rate of 2π, so no bank is too big for the rate.

    Remix prompts

    • Which concepts have a confident wrong intuition you could surface first? Heavier things fall faster; 0.999… is less than 1; multiplying always makes bigger.
    • Ask for confidence as well as a guess. A confident wrong answer is the most valuable one to break.
    • Follow the surprise with a second case (the basketball) so the rule, not the number, is what sticks.

    05Vote, argue, vote again

    Is 0.999… equal to 1?

    After Eric Mazur's Peer Instruction and ConcepTests at Harvard

    Vote first. Is 0.999… (nines forever) equal to 1?

    Two classmates disagree. Which argument convinces you more?

    Vote again.

    Why it works

    A ConcepTest is a conceptual question with tempting wrong answers. Learners commit, then argue with a neighbour before anyone gives the answer. Explaining your reasoning out loud exposes its weak joint. At Harvard, Mazur found this kind of interactive teaching roughly tripled gains on conceptual tests, and the learning lasted longer. Online, the “neighbour” can be a pair of written arguments, one sound and one subtly broken.

    Analogies to try

    “Infinitely many nines” isn't a process still running; it's a finished address on the number line. Breaks: learners often hear “forever” as “not done yet”, which is exactly the misconception.
    If two numbers differ, you can fit a third between them. Try to name a number between 0.999… and 1.

    Remix prompts

    • Write the broken argument first: it should be the one a smart learner would actually make.
    • Show the vote split before and after the argument, as a class would see it.
    • Other good ConcepTests: Is a square a rectangle? Can a function be continuous everywhere and smooth nowhere?

    06Let learners make the data themselves

    Flip until the noise settles

    After Daniel Kunin's Seeing Theory (Brown University)

    Why it works

    Seeing Theory was built on the idea that the real barrier in statistics isn't the maths but the missing mental image. Here, every flip is yours, so “probability” stops being a number on a page. Early on the line swings wildly. After hundreds of flips it calms down around the true chance: the law of large numbers, felt before it's stated.

    Analogies to try

    One person's opinion is noisy; a crowd's average is calm. Breaks: only if the people are independent. A crowd that copies each other doesn't average out.
    Footprints of a wobbly walker on a long beach: zoomed in they zigzag, zoomed out they make a straight line.

    Remix prompts

    • Ask for a prediction first: after 10 flips, how far from 50% could you be?
    • Hide the bias and let learners estimate it from their own flips, as a first taste of inference.
    • Show the gambler's fallacy: after 5 heads, does tails become “due”?

    07One slider, every representation linked

    One spring, three views

    After Haynes Miller's MIT Mathlets, used in MIT's differential equations course (18.03) since 2002

    Why it works

    The equation x″ + b·x′ + 4x = 0 lives in three places at once: the bouncing mass, the graph of position over time, and the two roots of r² + br + 4 = 0 in the complex plane. The Mathlets link each representation to the same controls, so moving one slider teaches the connections. Complex roots mean oscillation; the roots colliding at b = 4 is exactly the moment the bouncing stops.

    Analogies to try

    A car's shock absorbers: too little damping and the car bounces down the road, too much and it sinks back slowly, critical damping settles it at once. Breaks: real shocks are nonlinear; the model is a first approximation.
    A door closer that is too loose slams and swings; too stiff, it takes forever.

    Remix prompts

    • What other idea has three faces you could link: a sequence, its graph and its table?
    • Hide one view and ask learners to predict it from the other two.
    • For engineers: add a driving force and let resonance surprise them.

    08A picture that is the proof

    Odd numbers build squares

    A classic proof without words (collected in Roger Nelsen's Proofs Without Words, MAA), in the spirit of the visual proofs on Mathologer

    Why it works

    1 + 3 + 5 + … + (2n − 1) = n² can be proved by induction, but this picture makes it obvious. Each odd number is an L-shaped frame that wraps the square beneath it into the next bigger square: going from n² to (n + 1)² adds 2n + 1 tiles. The algebra becomes a description of something you can see.

    Analogies to try

    Each odd number is a new corner bracket, like the next layer of tree rings, but square. Breaks: rings are round; the corner shape is the whole point.
    Induction is building the square layer by layer: if every layer fits, the tower stands.

    Remix prompts

    • Which other sums are secretly shapes? Triangle numbers and staircases, or the sum of cubes as a square.
    • Show the picture without the formula and ask learners to write the formula.
    • Where would a picture lie? Build a “proof” that looks right but isn't, like the missing-square puzzle.

    09No lecture: a ladder of problems that discovers the rule

    How many handshakes?

    After the problem-based curriculum of Phillips Exeter Academy: no textbook, just sequences of problems that work from cases to general rules around the Harkness table

    Why it works

    Exeter's maths classes have no chapters. Students meet carefully ordered problems and re-discover the rules themselves, so each rung is easy given the one before. Nobody states n(n − 1)/2. By the last rung the learner has invented it, and remembers it because it's theirs.

    Analogies to try

    Each new arrival shakes hands with everyone already in the room, like a guest walking down a receiving line. Breaks: nothing, which is why it's a good one: it maps to 0 + 1 + 2 + … + (n − 1).
    Count every handshake twice (once from each hand), then halve: n(n − 1) ÷ 2.

    Remix prompts

    • Write a five-rung ladder for any formula you'd normally state. What is the smallest first step?
    • Plant a rung where the obvious pattern breaks, like points on a circle and regions: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, then 31.
    • Let two learners' different methods meet, as at the Harkness table.

    10Put the knobs inside the sentence

    A sentence you can drag

    After Bret Victor's explorable explanations and Nicky Case and Vi Hart's Parable of the Polygons

    If you save a month at a year for , you'll have . Only of that is money you put in; is interest earning interest.

    Drag a dashed number sideways, or focus it and use the arrow keys.

    Why it works

    Victor's reactive documents put the parameters inside the prose, so reading and experimenting are the same act. Drag the years from 10 to 40 and watch the interest go from a sliver to most of the total. Exponential growth is felt as “it runs away from me”, which no formula conveys.

    Analogies to try

    A snowball rolling downhill picks up snow in proportion to its size. Breaks: real snowballs hit trees; compounding at a fixed rate never stops.
    Doubling time: at 7% a year your money doubles about every 10 years (the rule of 72), so 40 years is four doublings, not four times.

    Remix prompts

    • Which formula would come alive as a draggable sentence? Try the speed of a falling object, or the half-life of medicine in the blood.
    • Pair the sentence with a picture that responds to the same knobs.
    • Let learners find the knob setting that makes a claim true, like “interest is half the total”.

    Analogy workbench

    Two or three analogies per idea, each with the place it breaks, plus a line you might write. An analogy you know the limits of is a tool; one you don't is a misconception waiting to happen. Filter by course.

    The craft, in seven rules

    1. Concrete before abstract. Start with a thing a learner can picture (a rope, a door, a ring) and let the symbol arrive as a name for what they already see.
    2. Say which part maps to which. “The host is the filter; the 98 goats are what it removes.” An analogy with unstated mappings invites learners to map the wrong parts.
    3. Name where it breaks, out loud. Saying “the onion analogy fails because onion layers have thickness” turns a weakness into a lesson about limits.
    4. Prediction before explanation. Ask for a guess, ideally with confidence, before showing anything. The surprise does the teaching.
    5. Exaggerate the parameter. A hundred doors, a planet-sized rope, a million flips. Extremes make the structure impossible to miss.
    6. Mix analogies only when each carries a different part. The rubber sheet shows the grid moving; the exchange rate shows the area. Two analogies doing the same job just compete.
    7. Wordplay has to carry meaning. “The derivative is the slope you'd see if you zoomed in until the curve gave up” is memorable because the joke is the definition.
